# Proposal Section 1: Project Objectives

Use this reference when drafting or reviewing Edexcel Proposal Section 1 / Project Objectives. The section should read like a truthful plan made by a capable school student at the start of an EPQ, not like a university dissertation abstract written after the research is complete.

## What the section should do

Project objectives should show:

- what the student intends to understand, explore, compare, investigate, or make;
- the main concepts, case, data, material, or creative problem that will be used;
- how the project will develop toward an answer, judgement, or outcome;
- one or two research or production skills the student intends to practise.

Keep the sequence aligned with the project:

`understand the issue -> explore competing explanations or options -> use evidence or develop the work -> reach a reasoned outcome -> reflect on skills`

This is a planning sequence, not a required number of bullets or an official mark formula. A short, coherent set is better than a long list of overlapping objectives.

## Three-prompt coverage gate

When the Proposal template presents these prompts, answer each one explicitly:

1. **What is the question you want to answer?** State one central research question, hypothesis, commission, or design brief. Do not make the assessor infer it from a later objective.
2. **What do you want to find out?** Explain the main issue, concepts, cases, data, interpretations, or production choices the student plans to explore, and how the evidence or development work will lead to an outcome.
3. **What do you want to learn how to do?** Name the research, technical, creative, or communication skills the student intends to practise, such as source evaluation, comparing arguments, using evidence, referencing, analysis, making, testing, or presentation.

If the form provides one text box, use three labelled paragraphs or clear opening sentences in this order. A list of objectives may support the answers, but it does not replace them. The question should remain singular and central; `find out` should describe content and investigation; `learn how to do` should describe skills rather than repeat the topic.

Run this gate before calling Section 1 complete. If one prompt is missing, ask for the missing student decision or mark it `[TO CONFIRM]`; do not infer a personal skill goal or a final research question from polished wording alone.

## Length and completeness

Use the current Edexcel/centre form and any stated local limit as the controlling requirement. There is no universal official word-count threshold in this reference. Judge content completeness before counting words:

- Around 180–200 English words can be sufficient when the three prompts, central question, scope, evidence/method route, intended outcome, and learning skills are all clear.
- For a more complex P301 literature-based project with several theories or case examples, around 230–270 words may give enough room to show the research path without becoming an introduction.
- Do not expand to 400–500 words merely to appear thorough. Definitions, detailed literature summaries, case histories, or a full argument belong in the later research and dissertation, not in Section 1.
- Do not invent extra theories, sources, cases, dates, or skill goals to reach a suggested length. If useful content is complete, stop; if a required decision is missing, ask for it.

Treat these ranges as drafting heuristics only. A shorter coherent answer is stronger than a longer answer with repetition, and a longer answer is not automatically higher quality.

## Proposal-stage voice and technical level

Calibrate language to the student's supplied writing and understanding:

- Keep necessary subject vocabulary such as a named theory, model, variable, technique, or genre. Academic precision should not be removed merely to make the text sound younger.
- Reduce the density of abstract research verbs and specialist meta-language when they make the response sound like a completed dissertation. Prefer clear verbs such as `understand`, `explore`, `compare`, `use`, `consider`, `develop`, and `reach a conclusion` when they accurately describe the plan.
- Do not stack many advanced concepts in one objective unless the student has already supplied them as a manageable starting scope. A term can be added later through source evaluation and Activity Log development.
- Preserve the student's first-person perspective where the form calls for it, for example `My main objectives for this project are to:`. Do not replace the student's voice with institutional or examiner language.

The target is not casual language. It is clear Level 3 student language with the necessary academic content.

## Plan versus finished-paper language

At Proposal stage, use future-oriented or exploratory wording:

- `I plan to use...`
- `I want to understand...`
- `I will explore whether...`
- `I will compare...`
- `I will consider when...`
- `I aim to reach a conclusion...`

Avoid presenting uncompleted work as settled fact:

- do not say that a theory has already been shown to be the strongest;
- do not claim that selected sources, cases, data, or findings exist unless supplied;
- do not write a final argument, result, or limitation into the Proposal as though it has already been established.

It is acceptable to state the intended judgement, such as `consider whether X explains the issue better than Y`, provided the result remains open. The objective should not predetermine the conclusion.

## A reliable objective pattern

When the student has provided enough facts, a useful pattern is:

```text
My main objectives for this project are to:

- understand the central issue using [relevant concepts or models];
- explore [a competing explanation, mechanism, interpretation, or design choice];
- compare [theories, methods, cases, or options] and consider their strengths and limitations;
- use [academic sources, secondary cases, data, materials, feedback, or testing] to examine how well the ideas work in practice;
- reach a reasoned conclusion or complete an outcome without assuming the result in advance;
- develop [one or two named research, technical, creative, or communication skills].
```

Do not fill the brackets with invented facts. Remove any line that does not serve the question, method, or outcome. For P301, secondary case evidence and literature synthesis may be appropriate; do not force primary data. For P302, include the planned investigation method, data route, or measurement where relevant. For P303/P304, adapt the pattern to rehearsal, production, testing, feedback, and the performance or artefact brief.

## Review checks

Before presenting Section 1 as final, check:

1. Every objective contributes to the title/question/brief and is not just a repeat of the topic.
2. The three form prompts are each answered explicitly and in a traceable order when they are present.
3. The objectives describe intentions and a research path, not a retrospective summary of the final paper.
4. The final judgement remains open and is supported by a realistic method and evidence route.
5. Technical terms are necessary, limited enough for the scope, and explainable by the student.
6. The wording sounds like this student at the beginning of the project, while remaining precise enough for Level 3 work.
7. The objectives can later generate truthful Activity Log entries about source choices, changes, problems, findings, and skill development.
8. The length supports completeness without repeating the question or turning Section 1 into an introduction.

If the original text is over-academic, return both the revised student-facing version and a short explanation of what was simplified and what technical content was deliberately retained. Do not describe the student as weak or make unsupported claims about authorship or AI use.

## Output discipline

For coaching, identify the current objective's purpose, what is too final or too dense, which subject terms should stay, and the next student decision. For direct generation, return the Section 1 text first, then `Evidence to verify` and `Facts still needed`. Keep provisional cases, sources, dates, and conclusions marked as such.
