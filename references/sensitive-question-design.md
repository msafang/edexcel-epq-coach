# Sensitive and Normative P301 Question Design

Use this reference when a proposed P301 topic involves law, public policy, ethics, medicine, rights, identity, religion, inequality, or another contested value. It extracts a reusable decision method for question design. It does not contain a list of prohibited topics and cannot guarantee an assessment outcome.

## Core principle

A controversial topic is not automatically unsuitable, and a neutral-sounding title is not automatically safe or high quality. Judge the proposed project by its research design and evidence chain:

`clear question -> defined scope -> appropriate evidence -> competing interpretations -> justified judgement`

Before giving reassurance, check the current Edexcel specification, centre guidance, safeguarding/ethics procedures, and the student's access to suitable evidence. Do not claim that the exam board will reward or penalise a topic solely because of its subject matter.

## Title and question stress test

For every candidate wording, check:

1. **Object:** Is the practice, policy, theory, intervention, institution, or case precise?
2. **Context:** Are the jurisdiction, population, period, and relevant social or legal setting defined?
3. **Criterion:** What does “effective”, “ethical”, “fair”, “better”, or “justified” mean, and what evidence could assess it?
4. **Answer space:** Can credible evidence support more than one interpretation or policy option?
5. **Neutrality:** Does the wording invite investigation, or does it tell the student what to prove?
6. **Terminology:** Are legal, medical, ethical, and everyday meanings kept distinct?
7. **Manageability:** Can the question be answered within the project time, word limit, and source access?

Do not treat a question beginning with “Should” as automatically weak or one beginning with “To what extent” as automatically strong. Compare candidate titles against the full checklist. A question can be normative and still be researchable if its criteria, evidence, and scope are explicit.

## Useful question shapes

Use these as design patterns, not templates to copy:

| Pattern | Strength | Main risk to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How is **X** regulated or understood in **Y**? | establishes context and definitions | may remain descriptive without an evaluative criterion |
| To what extent does **X** meet **criterion** in **context**? | makes evaluation visible | “criterion” may be vague or based on an unexamined value |
| Which of **A** and **B** better addresses **criterion** in **context**? | creates a clear comparison | may force unlike systems into a false choice or import one context's standards into another |
| What are the strengths and limitations of **A** and **B** for **stakeholders/outcomes** in **context**? | preserves balanced comparison | can become a list unless the student explains how the evidence will be weighed |

If a working hypothesis is used, label it as provisional and state how contrary evidence could change it. Never turn “I want to prove...” into an objective presented as neutral research.

## Risk patterns and corrections

| Risk | What it looks like | Corrective move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy commentary | judging a government, institution, or group without analysing evidence | define a question, criteria, and counterarguments; distinguish description from evaluation |
| Predetermined conclusion | selecting sources only to support the student's existing position | identify the strongest opposing argument before drafting; record source exclusions and changes |
| Value stated as fact | presenting a contested moral, medical, or social claim without attribution | attribute the claim, show the evidence, and explain its limitations |
| Foreign-model shortcut | treating another jurisdiction as the universal standard | use comparative material for a stated purpose and test contextual transferability |
| Legal overclaim | collapsing regulation, prohibition, enforcement, liability, contract, and criminality into one label | identify the exact legal instrument, actor, conduct, and jurisdiction; verify with authoritative material |
| Concept conflation | treating related subtypes, stakeholders, or outcomes as interchangeable | define, separate, and state exclusions before searching |
| Scope expansion | adding every medical, social, legal, and economic issue because the topic is important | retain only areas that answer the question and assign each a distinct role |
| Approval or safeguarding gap | involving sensitive personal data, vulnerable people, clinical activity, or unsafe methods without a plan | escalate to the supervisor/centre; redesign or use an appropriate literature route where necessary |

The correction is not to remove all disagreement. The goal is to make disagreement visible, evidence-based, and manageable.

## Evidence discipline for contested topics

Map each major claim to the type of evidence that can support it. Keep the roles distinct:

- official or legal material can establish a rule, policy, or formal position;
- peer-reviewed research can support a finding, interpretation, or scholarly disagreement;
- professional guidance can show a practice standard or expert position;
- case, stakeholder, or statistical material can illustrate consequences or lived conditions, subject to its own limitations;
- comparative material can provide contrast, but does not automatically prove what should happen in the learner's context.

Every major evaluative criterion should have evidence and a stated limitation. Every strong policy recommendation should be tested against at least one serious counterargument. Do not use a source's existence as proof of its claim, and do not treat an AI-generated citation, pasted link, or search snippet as verified until its bibliographic details, publisher, relevance, and access have been checked.

## Reassurance and escalation

When a learner asks whether a topic will “cause trouble”, answer in three parts:

1. state that topic sensitivity alone is not a reliable basis for predicting a mark;
2. identify the actual risks in the proposed wording, method, evidence, or approval route;
3. give a concrete redesign or verification action, and escalate centre-specific approval questions to the tutor or centre.

Do not provide legal, medical, safeguarding, or political certainty beyond the supplied and verified evidence. If the relevant current rule is not available, mark it `[TO CONFIRM]`.

## Proposal and Activity Log handoff

Pass the result into the project records without turning risk analysis into a final conclusion:

| Decision evidence | Destination |
| --- | --- |
| revised title, question, criteria, definitions, and exclusions | Proposal title/question and Section 1 objectives |
| genuine reason for choosing a contested issue | Proposal Section 2 |
| evidence areas, opposing positions, source checks, and comparison plan | Proposal Sections 3 and 4 |
| approval, privacy, safeguarding, or method contingency | risk and alternative fields |
| title revision, rejected framing, replaced source, or changed scope | Activity Log |

The final question may evolve as research progresses. Record why it changed, what evidence prompted the change, and how the new wording affects scope. Do not erase the original decision history or write the eventual conclusion back into the initial Proposal.

## Readiness gate

Do not recommend finalising the Proposal until:

- the question has a defined object, context, criterion, and manageable boundary;
- the wording leaves room for evidence to change the judgement;
- key terms and legal or policy claims have an identifiable verification route;
- competing interpretations and source limitations are planned;
- any ethics, safeguarding, privacy, or approval issue has an appropriate route;
- the student can explain why this is a research question rather than an advocacy statement.
