# P301 Topic Design and Paper Architecture

Use this reference when a P301 Dissertation topic is academically interesting but still broad, when the learner is mixing definitions with arguments, or when they need to connect topic selection to a manageable paper plan. This is a teaching framework, not an Edexcel-required chapter structure, word count, or mark scheme.

## Design sequence

Move through this sequence before treating the topic as Proposal-ready:

`interest or real-world issue -> working research question -> definitions and scope -> evidence areas -> paper architecture -> paragraph-source matrix -> Proposal and Activity Log handoff`

The question should remain open at the planning stage. A strong P301 design identifies what will be compared or evaluated without claiming that one explanation, policy, or outcome has already been proved.

## Narrowing a broad P301 issue

For a broad ethical, legal, policy, medical, social, or interdisciplinary issue, check the following in order:

1. **Object:** What exact practice, policy, theory, intervention, group, or case is being studied?
2. **Type or definition:** Are important subtypes being separated, rather than treated as one interchangeable category?
3. **Place and jurisdiction:** Which country, institution, market, community, or legal system is in scope?
4. **Time:** Is the project about a historical period, the current position, or a specified change over time?
5. **Criterion:** What will “to what extent”, “effective”, “ethical”, “successful”, or “better” mean in this project?
6. **Stakeholders and consequences:** Which interests or outcomes matter, and can the available literature address them?
7. **Exclusions:** Which related issues will not be studied so the project remains achievable?

If a question cannot answer most of these prompts, keep it as a topic candidate and do not generate a final Proposal. Avoid replacing a vague question with a large list of loosely related issues.

## Default P301 paper architecture

For a literature-based P301, use four broad parts as the default starting point. Keep the structure flexible and use only as many internal headings as help the reader follow the argument.

### 1. Introduction

The Introduction should:

- establish the real-world or academic background;
- define the terms that could otherwise cause confusion;
- state the jurisdiction, period, case, and exclusions;
- introduce the working research question and explain why it is worth investigating.

It should not announce the final answer or present a later finding as though it was known when the Proposal was written.

### 2. Literature Review

Organise this section around debates or evidence areas, not one paragraph per author. Show:

- what the existing literature argues;
- where sources agree, disagree, or use different assumptions;
- which sources are academic, official, professional, legal, or otherwise relevant;
- what each group of sources can and cannot establish for the question.

The Literature Review answers **what the existing evidence says**. It is not a catalogue of quotations and not yet the student's final judgement.

### 3. Discussion

Use the Discussion to compare and evaluate the evidence in relation to the question. The learner should:

- test competing explanations or interpretations;
- apply the evidence to the chosen jurisdiction, case, or criterion;
- consider counterarguments and alternative explanations;
- explain how source quality, date, perspective, legal context, or evidence limits affect the judgement;
- develop a qualified answer rather than merely repeat the Literature Review.

The Discussion answers **what the evidence means for this question**. Keep factual source summary and the student's reasoned evaluation visibly distinct.

### 4. Conclusion

Answer the research question directly and with an appropriate degree of certainty. State the conditions, limits, or distinctions that make the answer more accurate. Do not introduce new evidence, turn the conclusion into a generic reflection, or claim that the question has a simpler answer than the discussion supports.

This four-part structure is a student-friendly default for P301, not a requirement to copy it mechanically. P301 proposals should describe intended research and analysis; they should not pretend that the final chapter balance or conclusion is already known.

## Optional word planning

If the centre's confirmed target is approximately 5,000–5,600 words, the following is an internal planning example:

| Part | Internal planning range | Main purpose |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Introduction | 550–650 | background, definitions, scope, question |
| Literature Review | 1,500–1,700 | evidence areas and disagreements |
| Discussion | 2,500–2,700 | comparison, evaluation, and judgement |
| Conclusion | 450–550 | direct, qualified answer |

These ranges are not Edexcel thresholds. Follow the centre's actual limit and the needs of the question. Do not add paragraphs merely to reach a number or allow the Introduction to become a second Literature Review.

## Paragraph-source matrix

Before the learner begins full drafting, build a compact matrix. It is a planning and reading tool, not a substitute for the Activity Log or the final bibliography.

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Section / paragraph function | What this paragraph must do for the argument |
| Sub-question or claim | The precise issue being examined, not a vague topic label |
| Evidence needed | Academic debate, official rule, professional guidance, case evidence, data, or counterargument |
| Source lead | Title, author/body, year, and search result or provisional link |
| Why it belongs | Which part of the research question it helps answer |
| Limitation | Authority, currency, bias, jurisdiction, sample, method, or access limitation |
| Search terms | Two or three terms that can find a stronger or contrasting source |
| Status | Core, supplementary, replacement needed, or not yet verified |
| Next action | Read, compare, verify, replace, or record the decision in the Activity Log |

Use a small number of evidence areas with distinct roles. Do not add disciplines, cases, or source types just to make the matrix look broad. A case can illustrate or test an argument; it is not automatically a separate academic field.

## Source and link verification

Previous AI output, a pasted literature matrix, a search-result snippet, or a citation copied from another document is a lead, not verified evidence. Before recommending a source to a student:

1. confirm the exact title, author or issuing body, publication year, and publication venue;
2. open the publisher, official, DOI, PubMed, repository, or other authoritative page where possible;
3. check that the source actually supports the planned claim and is relevant to the chosen jurisdiction and time;
4. record access limits or the need for a backup source;
5. do not present a source as peer-reviewed, official, current, or full-text accessible unless that status has been checked.

If a link or bibliographic fact cannot be verified, mark it `[TO CONFIRM]` and give the learner a search task rather than inventing a replacement citation. Sources from another country may help with definitions or comparison, but they must not silently become evidence of the learner's chosen legal or policy context.

## Readiness and handoff

A P301 topic is ready to enter Proposal when the learner has:

- one working research question with a defined object and manageable scope;
- key terms, jurisdiction, period, and exclusions that are clear enough to guide searching;
- a small set of evidence areas with different purposes;
- at least one verified or verifiable source lead and one unresolved disagreement, limitation, or counterargument for the main areas;
- a planned paper architecture that separates Literature Review from Discussion;
- a method and outcome consistent with a literature-based P301;
- no predetermined conclusion presented as a fact.

Map the design into the Proposal as follows:

| Topic-design evidence | Proposal / later-record destination |
| --- | --- |
| question, definitions, scope, exclusions | title/question and Section 1 objectives |
| genuine interest and reason for this question | Section 2 |
| evidence areas, reading stages, matrix actions, review points | Sections 3 and 4 |
| source-access gaps and backup routes | resources, risks, and contingencies |
| rejected definitions, narrowed scope, source replacement, or changed interpretation | Activity Log |

Keep the matrix and decision changes as process evidence. Do not copy an eventual conclusion into the original Proposal, and do not turn an unverified source list into a claim that the student has completed the research.
