# Proposal Section 2: Reasons for Choosing the Project

Use this reference when drafting or reviewing the Proposal field about why the student chose the project. The section should make the student's motivation, subject connection, and reason for choosing the particular question visible. It should add information to Section 1, not repeat the research question, objectives, or skill list.

## Functional division from Section 1

Keep the two sections distinct:

| Section | Main question | What belongs there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Section 1 | What will I answer, find out, and learn how to do? | question/brief, research path, concepts or evidence, intended outcome, skills |
| Section 2 | Why did I choose this project? | personal interest, trigger or observation, subject/future connection, and why this particular problem is worth exploring |

If Section 1 already states source evaluation, referencing, or critical writing skills, do not repeat the same list in Section 2 unless it is connected to a specific personal reason. If the student has not supplied a future-study direction, use `my interests in...` rather than claiming `my future interests in...`.

## A three-paragraph structure

Use this sequence when it fits the form:

1. **Personal starting point:** explain a concrete interest, observation, experience, question, or encounter with material. Show why the student noticed the issue; do not invent a childhood story or personal experience.
2. **Subject and future connection:** connect the project to subjects, courses, skills, or a confirmed future direction. Explain the connection rather than merely naming two subjects.
3. **Why this particular project:** explain why the chosen question, case, problem, performance, or artefact is a useful way for this student to explore the connection. Show what the project adds beyond a general interest.

The paragraphs can be combined when the actual form is short. The structure is a teaching aid, not an official required format.

## Personal motivation without repetition

Move from a broad interest to the specific project:

`personal interest or observation -> specific tension/question -> subject connection -> reason this project is manageable and worth doing`

Avoid replacing motivation with generic claims such as “this is important to society” or “I have always been interested in business”. Ask what the student saw, read, studied, noticed, or wanted to understand, and why that led to this question. Keep the student's voice and level; improve clarity without manufacturing emotion or ambition.

## Theoretical and disciplinary accuracy

Check that the explanation of the student's interest does not create a false opposition between disciplines or theories. For example:

- A firm responding strategically to a competitor is not automatically behaving irrationally. Strategic responses can be part of rational intertemporal profit maximisation, even when they involve lower short-term profits for a longer-term objective.
- Behavioural explanations can complement rational models; do not describe “economic” and “psychological” explanations as mutually exclusive unless the student has evidence for that claim.
- When discussing a business decision, distinguish short-term loss, long-term profit, market share, entry deterrence, reputation, competitive pressure, and managerial emotion where the distinction matters.
- Preserve named concepts supplied by the student when they are relevant and explainable. Do not add a cluster of advanced theories merely to make the motivation sound academic.

When a claim is not yet established, use cautious wording such as `may`, `could`, `I became interested in whether`, or `I want to understand`. If the student has not supplied the theoretical basis, ask for it or mark it `[TO CONFIRM]`.

## Check future-study certainty

Use the strongest wording supported by the student's evidence:

- confirmed course, application, or future plan: `my future study in...` may be appropriate;
- stated subject interest without a confirmed plan: `my interests in...`;
- no supplied connection: ask the student rather than inventing one.

Do not turn a possible university major, career idea, or subject preference into a definite future plan. A truthful present interest is sufficient justification.

## Review and generation checks

Before presenting Section 2 as final, check:

1. A specific personal reason is visible and belongs to this student.
2. The connection to subjects or future interests is explained, not just named.
3. The reason for choosing this question/case/brief is distinct from the reason for liking the subject.
4. The text does not repeat Section 1's research question, objectives, evidence plan, or skill list without adding a personal reason.
5. Claims about rationality, psychology, business, short-term loss, and long-term aims are conceptually accurate and appropriately cautious.
6. Advanced terms are limited to what the student has supplied or can explain.
7. The wording reflects the student's certainty at the Proposal stage and does not read like a post-completion justification.

Use the existing quality scale (`Unanswered`, `Initial`, `Executable`, `Strong`) rather than assigning a fixed numeric score. A strong Section 2 is specific, truthful, personally connected, conceptually accurate, and non-repetitive; it is not necessarily longer or more sophisticated.

## Output modes

For coaching, identify the personal trigger, subject/future connection, project-specific reason, repetition risk, theoretical accuracy issue, and the next student question. For direct generation, return the student-facing Section 2 text, followed by `Evidence to verify` and `Facts still needed`. If rewriting an over-academic draft, briefly state which technical content was retained, which claims were softened, and why Section 1 content was removed or relocated.
